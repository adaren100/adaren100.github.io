**[What work can robots do?](https://www.anthropic.com/research/what-work-can-robots-do)** — _Anthropic · Sep 30_

**Main takeaway:** Anthropic built a robot exposure index measuring how well present-day robots can perform US job tasks, and finds robots can already do 74% of physical tasks, covering 34% of working hours — but they are cost-competitive with human labor for just 0.3% of tasks. At historical rates of robot price decline, it would take 40 years for that 0.3% to reach 10%.

**Main methods:**
- **Task-level exposure index built with Claude.** Robots are defined as autonomous physical machines that sense and act, and Claude is used to assess how well today's robots can perform each work task; a job counts as more exposed when robots can do more of its tasks in less controlled environments.
- **Capability and cost scored separately.** Capability exposure (74% of physical tasks, 34% of working hours) is kept distinct from cost-competitiveness, defined as a robot doing the same task cheaper than a human worker — only 0.3% of tasks clear that bar.
- **Robots and LLMs cover different work.** About 80% of job tasks by working time are exposed to robots or LLMs, with robots reaching work LLMs cannot. The unexposed fifth is either highly interpersonal or needs physical skills robots lack, such as the dexterity to untangle wires.
- **50-year backtest as validation.** From 1977 to today, jobs that were more exposed to the robots existing at the time saw larger wage and employment declines in later decades, which supports using present-day capability as a predictor.
- **Exposure creeps up ~2% a year.** Each year robots become able to do roughly 2% of the physical work they previously couldn't, and most still need highly structured environments like factories because they must be programmed for the physical world rather than adapting to it.
- **Who is exposed, and what else blocks adoption.** Exposed workers skew male, less educated, and lower paid; driving and warehouse jobs rank high while nursing and general repair rank low. Beyond cost and capability, human preferences and regulation rule out a significant share of tasks.

**[机器人现在到底能干哪些活？](https://www.anthropic.com/research/what-work-can-robots-do)** — _Anthropic · 9月30日_

**Main takeaway:** Anthropic 做了一个 robot exposure index，衡量现在的机器人能把美国的工作任务完成到什么程度。结论是物理类任务里 74% 机器人已经能做，占到 34% 的工作小时，但真正在成本上比人划算的只有 0.3%。按过去机器人降价的速度推，这 0.3% 要涨到 10% 得等 40 年。

**Main methods:**
- **用 Claude 在任务粒度上打分。** 他们把 robot 定义成能感知也能动作的自主物理机器，然后让 Claude 评估今天的机器人每项任务能做多好。一份工作的任务越多能被机器人拿下、而且越不依赖高度受控的环境，exposure 就越高。
- **能力和成本分开算。** 能力层面是 74% 的物理任务、34% 的工作小时；cost-competitive 是另一回事，指同一个任务机器人干得比人便宜，这一条只有 0.3% 的任务过得了。
- **机器人和 LLM 吃的是不同的活。** 按工作时间算，大约 80% 的任务要么暴露给机器人要么暴露给 LLM，机器人能碰到 LLM 碰不到的那部分。剩下那五分之一要么高度依赖人际互动，要么需要机器人现在还没有的手上功夫，比如把缠成一团的线理开。
- **拿 50 年数据做 backtest。** 从 1977 年到现在，当年对已有机器人 exposure 更高的工作，后面几十年工资和就业跌得更多。这说明用"今天机器人能做什么"来预测是站得住的。
- **每年往上爬 2%。** 机器人每年能多拿下大约 2% 以前做不了的物理工作。不过大部分机器人还是离不开工厂这种高度结构化的环境，因为它们得被编程才能跟物理世界打交道，不像人能自己适应。
- **谁受影响，以及还有什么卡着。** exposure 高的劳动者更偏男性、学历偏低、收入偏低；开车和仓库这类工作排在前面，护理和一般维修排在后面，因为后者就算在高度受控的环境里机器人也干不了多少。除了成本和能力，人的偏好和监管也挡掉了相当一部分任务。
